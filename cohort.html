Step 2 · Plain-language lesson

A software build starts when someone has a problem worth solving. Discovery means listening to users and seeing how work happens today. Scope is the written agreement about what the first release will do. Design shows the flow and screens before the engineering team makes them work.

Engineers then create the system. QA means checking it against agreed rules. Release means making it available to real users under a plan. Handoff gives the client access, instructions, records, and support contacts. Support covers fixes, questions, and later changes. Testing and feedback can happen during several phases, even when a chart draws them as a straight line.

Your place: clarify the business outcome in discovery, write back scope for approval, connect open decisions to owners, review QA evidence, keep the client informed, and check the handoff record. The technical lead owns implementation decisions; the client or delegated product owner approves business priorities and acceptance.

Illustrative startup or SMB case

Illustrative SMB: A local courier firm loses delivery proofs in chat threads. Discovery finds the broken step. First scope: drivers upload a proof photo, dispatch sees status, and managers export a daily report. The engineer designs storage and access. QA tests missing photos and wrong permissions. The firm checks real jobs before release.

Step 3 · Glossary

Discovery
Short research to learn the user problem and current process.
Scope
The written boundary of work to be delivered.
Design
A plan for screens, flows, and system structure.
Build
Engineering work that turns an agreed plan into working software.
QA
Checks that find defects and show whether requirements pass.
Release
Making a tested version available to users.
Handoff
Transfer of access, records, instructions, and ownership.
Support
Help with fixes and upkeep after release.

Step 4 · What I would say to a founder

“Before we plan a build, I want to see the work as your team does it today.”

“I will write back the scope, open questions, and success checks so you can confirm them.”

“Our engineering lead will own technical choices. I will keep decisions and test evidence clear for you.”

Step 5 · What I ask the engineer

  1. Which phase decisions need a client answer before engineering can proceed?
  2. What proof will show this release is ready for user testing?
  3. What access and documents must exist for a safe handoff?

Step 6 · 3-question check

1. Who approves the business outcome and priorities?
Reveal answer

The founder or delegated product owner. Engineering advises on what is feasible.

2. What is the difference between release and handoff?
Reveal answer

Release makes the product available. Handoff transfers knowledge, access, records and ownership.

3. What should Shohan do when an engineer reports a blocker?
Reveal answer

Record the blocker, owner, impact, decision needed, and next update. The engineer works on the technical fix.

Step 2 · Plain-language lesson

A business need names a problem and result: “Dispatch must know which parcels are late.” A requirement says what the system must do or how well it must perform. A functional requirement is an action: “Show overdue deliveries.” A non-functional requirement is a condition: “Only a supervisor can see customer phone numbers.” Speed, safety, uptime and access are also non-functional needs.

A user story is a small statement from one user’s view: “As a dispatcher, I want to filter late parcels so I can call the right driver.” Acceptance criteria are exact checks. For example: “Given a parcel is 30 minutes late, when dispatch opens Late, then the parcel appears with driver name and last update.” Also test the case where a parcel arrives on time.

Ask for one real example, one exception, and who makes each decision. Read the proposed rule back to the founder. Record assumptions as open questions until the right person confirms them. A good story is small enough to inspect, yet clear enough to test.

Illustrative startup or SMB case

Illustrative startup: A clinic booking founder asks for “easy scheduling.” You ask who books, what counts as an open slot, and who can cancel. The first story lets reception choose a vacant slot. Acceptance checks cover double booking, cancellation, time zone, and permission. Healthcare data handling needs separate review.

Step 3 · Glossary

Stakeholder

Business need
The problem the company wants to solve.
Functional requirement
An action the software must perform.
Non-functional requirement
A quality or limit, such as privacy or speed.
User story
A small need written from a user’s point of view.
Acceptance criteria
Specific checks that say whether a story passes.
Edge case
An unusual situation that still needs a rule.
Assumption
A belief that needs confirmation before it becomes scope.

Step 4 · What I would say to a founder

“Walk me through the last real time this problem happened.”

“Who must take action, what should happen next, and what exceptions should we expect?”

“I will send a short version in writing. Please correct the rules before we estimate.”

Step 5 · What I ask the engineer

  1. Which rules or missing data stop this story from being testable?
  2. What edge cases or security needs need a separate requirement?
  3. Can we split this into a smaller first release without losing the business result?

Step 6 · 3-question check

1. Is “the system should be fast” testable acceptance criteria?
Reveal answer

No. State an agreed measure, such as which screen, load and response target, then ask engineering to validate.

2. Write the three parts of a user story.
Reveal answer

As a [user], I want [action], so that [reason].

3. Who confirms an assumption about how the business works?
Reveal answer

The founder or authorized business process owner. The engineer checks technical feasibility.

Step 2 · Plain-language lesson

A prototype is an early model used to learn about an idea. It might be pictures or a clickable screen. An MVP is the smallest working version that can test a business assumption with real use. A full product usually includes more workflows, reports, edge cases and polish. “MVP” does not excuse weak security or unsafe data handling.

Scope should say what is included, excluded, assumed, and still undecided. Tie every included feature to a business outcome and acceptance check. Put nice-to-have items in a later list. A scope baseline is the version the buyer approves before delivery work is committed.

When a new request comes in, record a change request. The team checks impact on effort, schedule, risks and other features. The authorized buyer decides to approve it, trade it for something else, or leave it for later. A polite boundary keeps the record accurate.

Illustrative startup or SMB case

Illustrative restaurant startup: V1 lets customers place pickup orders and staff mark them ready. Delivery driver routing, loyalty points and advanced analytics are outside V1. Halfway through, the founder requests table reservations. You log it and ask engineering for impact before the founder decides.

Step 3 · Glossary

Prototype
A model used to test a concept or flow.
MVP
Smallest working release that tests a core assumption.
Full build
A wider product with more planned features and needs.
In scope
Work that is included in the agreement.
Out of scope
Work that is excluded from this phase.
Baseline
An approved starting version of scope.
Change request
A documented proposal to change agreed work.
Scope creep
Work expanding without clear review or approval.

“I will separate launch needs from later requests and show both for approval.”

“That addition may affect the current plan. I will get a technical impact review before we agree to it.”

  2. Which dependencies make this feature larger than it looks?
  3. What changes in testing or timeline if this request is approved?

Reveal answer

No. A design can test a flow; working payments need an implemented and tested integration.

2. Name four parts of a scope boundary.
Reveal answer

Inclusions, exclusions, assumptions, and open decisions, with acceptance checks where possible.

3. Who approves a change after technical impact is known?
Reveal answer

The authorized buyer or agreed product owner, under the project change rule.

Step 2 · Plain-language lesson

Waterfall usually plans one phase before the next. It works best when the requirements are stable and formal sign-offs matter. Agile is a set of values that favors regular working results, close customer feedback, and adjustment. Scrum is one way to work in short periods called sprints. A sprint has a clear goal and lasts one month or less under the Scrum Guide.

The product backlog is an ordered list of work the product may need. The Product Owner is accountable for making it useful and ordered. The Scrum Master supports how Scrum is used. The developers decide how to turn selected work into a finished result. In Scrum, “developers” means the people making that result and can include different skills; it is not your job title.

Kanban shows work moving through steps and limits how much work is active. Lean asks what creates value and what wastes effort. Estimates express uncertainty, not a promise. Velocity is a team-specific past measure; it should never be sold as a guarantee or compared as a score between teams. A founder should hear the sprint goal, visible evidence, decisions needed, and forecast risks.

Illustrative startup or SMB case

Illustrative agency: A client needs a compliance form with fixed approval steps, so early review may be formal. A new customer dashboard has uncertain user behavior, so the team reviews small releases every two weeks. Support fixes flow through a Kanban board with urgent work rules.

Step 3 · Glossary

Waterfall
A phase-by-phase plan with formal handoffs.
Agile
Values for learning through working results and feedback.
Scrum
A team framework organized around short sprints.
Sprint
A fixed period of up to one month to pursue a goal.
Backlog
Ordered list of possible product work.
Kanban
A method for visualizing and improving flow.
WIP limit
A cap on work started at the same time.
Velocity
A team’s past count of estimated work completed per sprint.
Lean
A way to focus on value and remove wasted effort.

Step 4 · What I would say to a founder

“You will see a clear goal for the next work period and what passed review.”

“I will keep your decisions, risks, and scope changes in one readable record.”

“The engineering team will forecast work from evidence and uncertainty, then update that forecast as we learn.”

Step 5 · What I ask the engineer

  1. What is the next sprint goal or flow priority, and how will we show it?
  2. Which backlog items lack acceptance criteria?
  3. What might change the forecast, and when will we know more?

Step 6 · 3-question check

1. Does Agile mean the client can add anything without a scope decision?
Reveal answer

No. Agile welcomes learning, while contracts and change rules still define commercial commitments.

2. What is the official maximum Scrum sprint length?
Reveal answer

One month or less, per the 2020 Scrum Guide.

3. Why can velocity mislead a buyer?
Reveal answer

It is based on one team’s estimates and past work, so it is not a cross-team score or fixed promise.

Step 2 · Plain-language lesson

Architecture is a map of the system’s parts and how they connect. The frontend is the screen a person uses. The backend checks rules and performs work. A database stores records. An API is an agreed way for two software parts to exchange requests and answers. An integration connects your product to another provider, such as payments or a courier tracking feed.

Cloud hosting means renting computing resources through a provider rather than running your own physical servers. Security starts with identity, access rules, safe data handling, backups, logs and update plans. Scalability asks what happens as usage grows. “We might reach a million users” is not enough to choose expensive architecture; ask for expected users, busiest traffic and growth triggers.

A monolith keeps much of a product in one deployable system. Microservices divide functions into smaller services. Each choice has costs. A well-structured monolith can suit an early product because it is simpler to operate. Microservices can help at larger scale but add network, monitoring and coordination work. The CTO or engineering lead owns the recommendation.

Illustrative startup or SMB case

Illustrative courier SMB: A dispatcher clicks “Delivered.” The frontend sends a request through an API. The backend checks that the worker may update that job. The database saves the proof and time. A notification service informs the customer. The architecture review asks what happens if the notification service is down.

2. Are login and permission the same?

3. Why might an early product use a monolith?

Step 2 · Plain-language lesson

A technology stack is the set of tools used to make and run software. A web app runs through a browser. A mobile app is installed on a phone. Native apps are made for one phone platform. Cross-platform tools let a team share much of the app work across iOS and Android. A SaaS product is software that customers use as an ongoing online service, often paid by subscription.

You may hear React, Next.js, Node.js, .NET, Python, Flutter, PostgreSQL, AWS or Azure. Their names describe tool families, not a result. React and Next.js relate to web interfaces; Node.js and .NET can run server logic; PostgreSQL stores structured data; Flutter supports apps across platforms; AWS and Azure provide cloud services. The full choice depends on the team’s skill, product needs, existing systems, security and upkeep.

Third-party services can save development time for login, maps, payments or messages. They also create costs, contract limits and provider risks. The safe business question is who owns the accounts and data, and what happens if the service changes its price or rules.

Illustrative startup or SMB case

Illustrative gym startup: Members book classes on a web app. Coaches also want phone push messages. The team may recommend a responsive website first, then a mobile app when phone-only actions justify it. You ask for the business tradeoff and keep the choice in a decision record.

Step 2 · Plain-language lesson

A large language model (LLM) predicts useful text from an input. A product can send a request to an LLM through an API. That enables features such as drafting a reply, sorting a support ticket or summarizing records. Model output can be wrong, so important claims and actions need checks.

An agent is a software setup that can choose steps and use allowed tools to carry out a task. Model Context Protocol (MCP) is a standard way an AI application can connect to outside data and tools. Automation tools like n8n, Zapier and Make link events to actions using workflows. Each tool has its own permissions and failure paths. An LLM feature, an agent, and a rule-based workflow should be scoped separately.

Before approving an AI feature, ask what data enters the model, whether the provider stores it, what rules govern retention, how permission is enforced, what records are kept, and who reviews errors. Ask how much each request costs and what happens when the model is unavailable. Never promise legal or security compliance from a product name alone.

Illustrative startup or SMB case

Illustrative HVAC SMB: An inbox assistant drafts replies to service requests. The first release may classify the message and prepare a draft. A human approves every outbound reply. The system keeps an action log, hides customer details where possible, and routes uncertain cases to staff.

How long a provider or system keeps data.

  3. How will the workflow behave if the model, tool or vendor fails?

Step 2 · Plain-language lesson

Effort depends on more than the number of screens. Business rules, data cleanup, roles, integrations, security, old systems, test coverage, and release work all take time. A feature estimate is a forecast based on assumptions. The technical team may give a range or split discovery from delivery when too much is unknown.

Fixed scope pricing works when outcomes and boundaries are clear enough to agree on the work. The supplier carries more risk if the agreed work takes longer than expected, so the assumptions and change terms matter. Time and materials charges for time and agreed costs as work goes on. It allows changing priorities but needs a spending limit, regular evidence and approvals. Milestones mark measurable results or acceptance points; payment terms must be agreed commercially.

Your job is to collect the business details, write what is included and unknown, request engineering effort analysis and route commercial terms for approval. Never give a client a number or delivery guarantee for Codezzi without an authorized quote. A responsible next step is discovery when the estimate is too uncertain.

Step 2 · Plain-language lesson

QA is the work that keeps quality visible. A unit test checks a small part; an integration test checks parts working together; an end-to-end test checks a full user flow. Regression testing checks that old work still passes after a change. Security testing checks unsafe access or data use. Performance testing checks how the system behaves under the expected load.

User acceptance testing (UAT) is when the buyer or real users test agreed business cases and confirm fit. A test record should show the requirement, steps, expected result, actual result, pass or fail, issue owner and date. A failed test needs a clear plan and decision; a verbal “all good” is weak evidence.

For handoff, ask for source and access transfer as contracted, setup instructions, system diagram, data backup and recovery steps, integration owner list, open issues, release notes, training material and support terms. The client should be able to operate the product or pass it to another team without relying on one person’s memory.

Illustrative property manager: A tenant can submit a repair ticket. Tests check submission, manager approval, email alerts, duplicate tickets and wrong-role access. UAT uses real staff cases. Handoff includes the process guide, admin access and a list of open items.

Step 2 · Plain-language lesson

Sales Navigator is a LinkedIn research tool. A saved search remembers filters and can show new matches. A lead list holds people you choose to track. An account list holds companies. Alerts flag changes or posts worth checking. InMail is a way to write to some people outside your connections, subject to your plan and credits.

Smart Links bundle shared content and may show engagement. TeamLink can show teammates connected to a prospect. LinkedIn lists these as features of higher plans, so check your actual plan before expecting them. You already use saved searches and lists; keep your workflow there. A signal, such as hiring or a founder posting about an internal tool, is a reason to research, not proof of buying intent.

Daily manual routine: choose one ideal buyer group; revisit a saved account or lead search; read a few relevant profiles and company updates; save qualified leads to a small named list; check for overlap and prior contact; write one personal note only when there is a real reason. Track the person, problem signal, source date, action and next follow-up in your own permitted records. Never scrape, auto-message, bulk export, or use an unauthorized automation tool.
